Executive Summary

ABOUT THIS SCORECARD

This report measures how today's leading AI models handle African languages using standard, publicly available tests — nothing more, nothing less.

What this scorecard does not use:
AfroEval's proprietary architecture, its SME-authorized benchmark library, its scoring methodology, its data pipelines, its Human-in-the-Loop review processes, or its governance model. None of AfroEval's intellectual property is present in this evaluation.

What the scores mean:
These numbers show how frontier models perform on African languages under generic conditions — the same conditions most organizations use today when they deploy AI without African-specialized evaluation infrastructure.

What the scores do not mean:
They are not a certified readiness assessment. They are not AfroEval scores. They are not a substitute for African-specific evaluation. That is precisely the gap AfroEval is built to close.

Across 8 African languages and 7 performance dimensions, frontier models scored between 87.0% and 98.7% on generic multilingual tests. These scores reflect what current models can do on broadly-designed evaluations. They do not reflect how the same models perform when assessed against African-specific knowledge, cultural context, and community-level language use — which requires a different kind of measurement entirely.

Scores tend to be highest for higher-resource languages (such as Swahili and Hausa) and show more variation for lower-resource languages (such as Af-Soomaali and Afaan Oromoo). The Language-by-Language view below shows this gradient.

About these benchmarks

These are the African-language NLP benchmarks the field uses to measure how well AI models handle African languages — most from the African NLP research community (notably Masakhane) and related academic efforts. This scorecard authors its own original items inspired by their task formats; it does not use the datasets themselves.

BenchmarkTask typeWhat it measuresBaseline Model Scorecard it maps to
AfriMCQAMultiple-choice, cultural knowledgeWhether the model knows African cultural context — customs, institutions, geography, traditionsDimension 2 — Cultural Reasoning
AfriMMLUMultiple-choice, general knowledgeAfrican-language version of the MMLU exam (broad academic / factual knowledge); part of IrokoBenchDimension 3 — Knowledge & Reasoning
MasakhaNEWSText classificationSorting African-language news headlines into topics (business, health, politics, sports, etc.)Dimension 4 — News Classification
AfriMedQAMedical question-answeringAnswering basic health / medical questions accurately and safely in African contextsDimension 5 — Medical / Health QA
They're the academic/community “standardized tests” for African-language AI; our scorecard borrows their task formats to author original items, and cites their published results separately as an outside reference point.

Methodology Note

What was tested: 7 standard, publicly recognized dimensions — comprehension, cultural reasoning, knowledge & reasoning, news classification, medical/health QA, code-switching, and bias/disparity — all zero-shot with no African-specific tuning. Items are original, authored by AgentifyAfro and inspired by the task formats of public benchmarks; they are not drawn from or affiliated with those benchmarks.

This Generic Baseline Scorecard is produced solely using publicly available multilingual benchmarks and standard commercial model APIs. No component of AfroEval's intellectual property is incorporated herein, including but not limited to: its proprietary evaluation architecture, SME-authorized benchmark corpus, test suite design, scoring and weighting methodology, data ingestion and processing pipelines, Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) quality processes, and AI governance model.

The scores and observations in this report reflect frontier model behavior on African languages as encountered under generic, non-specialized evaluation conditions only. They do not represent, approximate, or substitute for performance measurements produced under AfroEval's certified evaluation framework. No claim is made regarding African-language deployment readiness.

AfroEval Scorecard™ and its evaluation methodology are proprietary to AgentifyAfro.

Why generic tests produce higher scores: generic multilingual tests reward broad capability and surface fluency, not African-specific accuracy, cultural context, or community-level language use. Scores are therefore structurally biased upward relative to an African-specialized evaluation.

Why the distinction matters: a high generic score does not establish that a model is fit for African-language deployment. That determination requires a measurement framework built for the context.

Judging: open-ended dimensions are scored by a neutral third-party model that is never one of the models being scored, at temperature 0. See docs/methodology.md for the full disclosure.
